# 11. What Level Does This Actually Take You To?

> **Status**: Review after 20 stories. Measured, not aspirational.
> **Short answer**: a solid **A1+**, with **A2-level survival in the 20 covered situations** and **A2+ mechanical skill at the keyboard**. Not A2 overall — the gaps are listening speed, tone production, and free conversation, which more stories cannot close.

---

## 1. What is actually in the app now

| Component | Amount |
|---|---|
| Typing curriculum | 300 words, 15 phases, Kedmanee keystroke sequences |
| Lessons | 10 lessons (letters, leading/vertical vowels, tone rules, clusters) |
| Stories | **20 scenes × 8 lines = 160 dialogue lines** |
| Deck words used inside stories | 133 / 300 (44 %) |
| Story-only words (declared, glossed) | 154 |
| **Total distinct vocabulary in the app** | **≈ 450 words** (300 typed + 154 contextual) |
| Grammar patterns covered | 15 / 15 core patterns |
| Art | 20 covers + 160 scene cards |

---

## 2. Skill-by-skill estimate (CEFR)

| Skill | Level | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Kedmanee typing** | **A2 / B1-equivalent mechanical skill** | You can type 300 high-frequency words from sound, including tone marks and leading vowels. Most A1 learners cannot type Thai at all — this is the app's genuine spike. |
| **Reading (script)** | **A1** | You recognise the taught letters and can decode familiar words; connected text still needs the token breakdown. The stories *are* graded reading practice. |
| **Listening** | **A1+ at slow, clear speed; below A1 at native speed** | TTS at 0.85× is clean and slow. Real Thai conversation is ~2× faster with elision. This is the biggest gap. |
| **Speaking (production)** | **A1** | You can produce memorised frames and answer questions in the 20 situations; you cannot yet generate novel sentences fluently. |
| **Vocabulary** | **A1+** | ~450 recognisable words vs ~500 for A1, ~1,500 for A2. |
| **Grammar** | **A1 solid + 4 A2 features** | 15 patterns including conditionals (ถ้า), comparatives (กว่า/ที่สุด/เกินไป), benefactive (ให้), ability (…ได้). |

**Overall: A1+, with domain spikes.**

CEFR's own A1 descriptor is: *"Can interact in a simple way provided the other person talks slowly and is prepared to help."* That is precisely the situation the app trains — and story 16 exists to teach the phrases that keep that interaction alive.

---

## 3. What a learner can actually do after all 20 scenes

- **Buy things** — prices, bargaining, sizes, colours, quantities by weight, change.
- **Eat** — order, say "not spicy", complain politely, ask for a replacement.
- **Move** — taxi fares, "how do I get to…", straight/left/right, distance and time.
- **Book** — rooms, per-night prices, "is there hot water", "could I have…".
- **Health** — say what hurts (`ปวด` vs `เจ็บ`), follow a dosage (`วันละสามครั้งหลังอาหาร`).
- **Social** — introduce a partner, age, work, compliment, invite someone out, small talk about weather/weekends, make plans.
- **Repair** — admit you don't understand, ask someone to slow down, repeat, or explain a word.
- **Talk about language itself** — type, read, listen, understand, "why do Thai people speak so fast".

That is roughly the top 20 situations a short trip or a new friendship actually produces.

---

## 4. What they still cannot do

1. **Follow natural-speed speech.** Nothing in the app plays Thai at native tempo.
2. **Produce tones reliably.** Speech Lab measures pitch against tone contours, but the stories don't assess production.
3. **Hold an unscripted conversation.** You can answer and initiate within the patterns; you can't yet improvise.
4. **Read connected text.** Word-level reading with breakdowns ≠ reading a page.
5. **Write from scratch.** Typing taught words ≠ spelling an unknown word.

---

## 5. Why the last five stories were these five

After 15 stories the grammar matrix was full, so the marginal value shifted from *patterns* to *functions* — the glue that turns isolated phrases into a conversation:

| Story | Function |
|---|---|
| 16. I Don't Understand | **repair** — keeps you in the conversation when it outruns you |
| 17. Small Talk at the Café | **social** — weather, weekend, "shall we get coffee" |
| 18. Making Plans | **scheduling** — meet, time, place, call to cancel |
| 19. At the Fresh Market | **quantities** — kilos, bottles, bags, change |
| 20. Lost in the City | **wayfinding** — "I'm lost", how to get there, thank a stranger |

These five add the phrases you need to *recover* and *continue*, which is exactly what separates "I know some Thai" from "I can get by".

---

## 6. What would actually take a learner to A2

Not more stories — stories have hit diminishing returns at 20 (deck coverage went 38 % → 44 % for five more scenes).

1. **Listening ladder** — the same 160 lines at 1.0×, 1.2×, then native-speed recordings with a "hide the Thai" mode. Biggest single win.
2. **Sentence building** — drag/tap word chips to assemble a line from English, with the keyboard as the input method. Converts recognition into production.
3. **Tone production drills** — extend Speech Lab from single syllables to whole story lines.
4. **Vocabulary to ~1,000** — the 300 deck plus the 154 story words is a good A1 core; A2 needs roughly double.
5. **Reading mode** — show a whole scene as unbroken Thai text, then reveal the breakdown on tap.

With those, A2 is realistic. Without them, adding stories 21–30 would raise vocabulary coverage but leave the three real gaps untouched.

---

## 7. Bottom line

> After memorising everything in this app, a learner is a **strong A1+ speaker**: competent in ~20 everyday situations, able to read short Thai words and type 300 of them from sound, able to ask for help when language fails — but not yet conversational at natural speed, and not A2 overall.

That is a genuinely useful place to be. It is the difference between a tourist who points at a menu and one who orders, asks for the bill, and jokes about the rain — and it is an honest, defensible A1 portfolio rather than a course that promises B1 and delivers phrases.
